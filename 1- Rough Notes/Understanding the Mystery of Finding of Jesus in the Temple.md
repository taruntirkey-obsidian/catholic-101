#### *Luke 2:41-42 mentions Jesus went with His parents for Passover when He was 12. Why He didn't go before the age of 12?*

**Jesus actually did travel to Jerusalem for Passover before the age of 12.** The text in **Luke 2:41** explicitly states that ==His parents went to Jerusalem **"every year"** for the Festival of the Passover==. In Jewish custom, families traveled together in large caravans, meaning Jesus likely accompanied Mary and Joseph on these annual trips throughout His childhood.

The text highlights His age of 12 specifically due to its cultural, legal, and developmental significance, rather than to imply it was His first trip.

Why the Age of 12 is Highlighted

- **Transition to Religious Accountability**: In first-century Jewish tradition, the age of 12 was the threshold of adolescence and the formal preparation period for adulthood. At age 13, a Jewish boy became a _Bar Mitzvah_ (a "son of the commandment"), making him personally responsible for keeping the Mosaic Law. Age 12 was the critical preparatory year to learn under the temple teachers. 
- **Shift from Custom to Legal Obligation**: Prior to this age, Jesus attended Passover as part of His family's personal devotion. At 12, the trip shifted from a family custom to a formal, personal obligation to prepare for adult religious duties.
- **The Revelation of His Divine Identity**: Luke focuses on this specific year because it serves as a milestone where Jesus publicly demonstrates His divine wisdom. Sitting among the scholars, He declares His awareness of His true identity by calling the Temple "My Father's house," signaling a transition from obedience to His earthly parents to His heavenly mission.

#### *Luke 2:49, Jesus answers as if it would be obvious for His parent to understand the situation. Does it have a deeper meaning?*

==Yes, Jesus's answer in **Luke 2:49** has a profound theological meaning==. When He asks, _"Why were you searching for me? Didn’t you know I had to be in my Father’s house?"_, He is not being disrespectful. Instead, He is gently pointing out a truth that His parents should have already known based on the miraculous circumstances of His birth.

The deeper meaning of His response breaks down into three key areas:

1. The Redefinition of "Father"

- **The Context**: Just two verses earlier, Mary says to Jesus, _"Your father and I have been anxiously searching for you."_ She is referring to Joseph.
- **The Pivot**: Jesus immediately redirects the word "father" away from Joseph and toward God.
- **The Meaning**: This is the very first time in the Gospels that Jesus speaks. His first recorded words are used to establish His divine sonship, declaring that His ultimate allegiance belongs to God, not an earthly family.

2. The Divine "Must" (_Dei_)

- **The Greek Word**: Jesus uses the Greek word _dei_ (translated as "had to" or "must").
- **The Meaning**: In the Gospel of Luke, this word is used exclusively to describe things that are **divinely necessary** for salvation history. Jesus is stating that being at the Temple was not a childhood whim or a mistake; it was an absolute, cosmic necessity dictated by His mission.

3. A Reminder of Their Calling

- **The Obviousness**: Jesus answers as if it should be obvious because both Mary and Joseph had received direct angelic visitations (Gabriel to Mary, and an angel in a dream to Joseph) detailing exactly who Jesus was.
- **The Meaning**: Jesus is gently reminding them of His true identity. He is essentially asking, _"Knowing who I am, where else did you think I would be?"_ It highlights the contrast between His clear focus on His divine mission and His parents' very human, understandable worry.

#### *Why Mary and Joseph failed to understand His response (as noted in verse 50)?*

To understand why Mary and Joseph did not understand Jesus’s response in **Luke 2:50**, we have to look at the massive gap between their human expectations and Jesus's divine reality. Even though they knew He was the Messiah, this moment caught them completely off guard.

Their lack of understanding stems from several key cultural, psychological, and spiritual reasons:

1. The Confusion of Roles (Parent vs. Disciple)

For 12 years, Mary and Joseph had lived a normal, quiet life in Nazareth raising a child. They were used to being the authorities, protectors, and teachers. Suddenly, the roles reversed. Jesus was the one teaching the religious experts, and He spoke to His parents with a new, independent authority. It is psychologically difficult for any parent to instantly transition from seeing their child as a dependent to seeing them as a divine authority.

2. A Different Concept of the Messiah

First-century Jews expected the Messiah to be a political, military king who would overthrow Roman oppression. No one expected the Messiah to be a 12-year-old boy quietly debating theology with rabbis in the Temple. Mary and Joseph knew Jesus was destined for greatness, but they did not yet understand _how_ that destiny would unfold. His actions did not fit their cultural blueprint of the Messiah.

3. The Unprecedented Claim of "Father"

In ancient Israel, Jewish people viewed God as the Father of the _nation_ of Israel as a whole, but individual Jews rarely called God "my Father" in a personal, intimate way. When Jesus used this term, it was radically new. Mary and Joseph likely struggled to grasp the literal, unique nature of Jesus’s divine sonship in that exact moment.

4. Human Grief and Relief

Psychologically, Mary and Joseph had just spent three agonizing days searching for a lost child in a crowded city. They were exhausted, terrified, and operating out of pure parental instinct. When they finally found Him, their emotional relief and lingering panic made it difficult to process a deep, abstract theological statement.

5. Divine Timing and Gradual Revelation

In the Gospels, God’s plan is revealed gradually, not all at once. Luke notes that while they didn't understand it then, Mary **"treasured all these things in her heart"** (**Luke 2:51**). This indicates that while the immediate meaning was hidden from them, they held onto His words, and the full understanding came much later—after His resurrection.

#### *How Jesus responded to them immediately after this misunderstanding (verse 51 and 52)?*

Following the misunderstanding, Jesus returned to Nazareth and was submissive to his parents, fulfilling his role as a child. He entered a period of quiet preparation, living a normal life, and grew physically, intellectually, spiritually, and socially, according to **Luke 2:51–52**.

#### *Was it customary for a Jewish boy to work at his father's or take up his work?*

==Yes, **it was highly customary and legally expected** for a Jewish boy to learn his father’s trade==. In first-century Jewish culture, a father had specific, formal obligations to his son. Along with circumcising him and teaching him the Torah, the father was required to **teach him a practical craft** to ensure he could support a family.

This societal expectation was deeply embedded for several reasons:

1. The Legal and Religious Mandate

Teaching a son a trade was not just a practical choice; it was viewed as a moral obligation. The _Talmud_ (the collection of ancient rabbinic teachings) states, _"He who does not teach his son a craft teaches him to be a robber"_. The logic was simple: if a young man had no honest way to earn a living, he would eventually be forced to steal to survive.

2. The Educational System and the "Yoke"

A boy's path was generally determined by his academic success in the local _Bet Sefer_ (synagogue elementary school).

- **The Top Students**: The most brilliant students would continue their religious studies and hope to be chosen by a prominent Rabbi as a disciple.
- **The Majority of Boys**: Those who did not pursue advanced rabbinic tracks finished formal school around age 12 to 15. They immediately entered a **formal apprenticeship under their father** to master the family trade.

3. High Value on Manual Labor

Unlike Greek or Roman cultures, which often looked down on manual labor as work fit only for slaves, Jewish culture held craftsmanship in high esteem. Even the most famous, highly educated rabbis maintained manual trades to remain self-sufficient. For example, the great scholar Hillel was a woodcutter, and the Apostle Paul (who studied under the elite scholar Gamaliel) was a trained tentmaker.

Application to Jesus

This cultural custom directly explains Jesus’s life before His public ministry. Joseph was a _tekton_ (traditionally translated as a carpenter, though it broadly means a builder or craftsman in wood and stone). Because Jesus submitted to His earthly parents after the Temple incident (**Luke 2:51**), He followed this exact cultural script. He didn't just work _with_ Joseph; He absorbed the trade entirely. By adulthood, the locals in His hometown didn't just know Him as "the carpenter's son" (**Matthew 13:55**)—they referred to Jesus Himself as **"the carpenter"** (**Mark 6:3**).
