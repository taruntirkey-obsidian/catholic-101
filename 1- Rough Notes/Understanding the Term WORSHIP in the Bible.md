## Words Used in Joshua 7:6 and 2 Chronicles 29:20
The specific Hebrew word used in both actions is shachah (שָׁחָה). [1, 2]

While it is the primary Hebrew word translated into English as "worship", its literal definition is "to bow down," "to prostrate oneself," or "to fall face down to the ground." [1, 2, 3]

The word appears in both of your passages, though it may look different depending on your English translation:

- Joshua 7:6 — "Then Joshua tore his clothes and fell to the earth upon his face (shachah) before the ark of the Lord..." Here, the word acts as a vivid description of physical prostration and deep mourning. [4, 5]
    
- 2 Chronicles 29:28–30 (The worship service organized by Hezekiah in verse 20) — "...the king and everyone present with him knelt down and worshiped (shachah)... they sang praises with gladness and bowed down and worshiped (shachah)." In this context, it describes corporate religious adoration. [6, 7, 8]
    

(Note: If you were thinking of the verse where the people bowed down to both God and a human king at the same time, that occurs in 1 Chronicles 29:20, where the exact same word, shachah, is used: "...and all the assembly blessed the Lord... and bowed down [shachah] their heads and prostrated themselves before the Lord and the king.") [9, 10]

## Worship used in the Bible Other than God
The original languages of the Bible use terms translated as "worship" that possess a broader range of meaning than the modern English word. In the ancient world, the core biblical words for worship literally meant to bow down, do obeisance, or prostrate oneself as an act of deep submission and respect. [1, 2, 3]

Consequently, the Bible frequently describes people "worshipping" individuals in authority—such as kings, prophets, and superiors—as an act of civil honor rather than divine adoration. [4, 5]

---

#### The Hebrew Word: Shachah (שָׁחָה)

In the Old Testament, the primary word for worship is shachah, which means to bow down low or stretch oneself out on the ground. [3, 6]

- To a King: In [1 Chronicles 29:20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Chronicles+29:20&version=NIV), the entire assembly of Israel "bowed down their heads, and worshipped the LORD, and the king." The same physical posture (shachah) was given simultaneously to God as divine worship and to King David as royal honor. [4, 7, 8]
    
- To a Brother: In Genesis 33:3, Jacob bows down (shachah) to the ground seven times before his brother Esau out of respect and to seek peace. [9, 10]
    
- To Strangers / Foreigners: In Genesis 23:7, Abraham stands up and bows down (shachah) to the local Hittites while purchasing a burial plot. [5, 11, 12]
    
- To a Prophet: In 2 Kings 2:15, the sons of the prophets recognize Elisha's authority and bow down (shachah) to the ground before him. [13]

Modern translations usually render shachah as "worship" when directed at God, and as "paid homage," "bowed low," or "did obeisance" when directed at a human ruler to avoid confusing readers. [14, 15, 16]

---

#### The Greek Word: Proskuneo (προσκυνέω)

In the New Testament, the dominant word for worship is proskuneo, which literally means to fall upon one's knees or touch the ground with the forehead as a token of profound reverence. [14, 17, 18, 19]
- Parable of the Unforgiving Servant: In Matthew 18:26, the servant falls on his knees and "worshipped" (proskuneo) his master, begging for extra time to pay back his debts.
    
- The Message to Philadelphia: In Revelation 3:9, Jesus states that He will force false accusers to come and "worship" (proskuneo) before the feet of the faithful church to show that God has loved them.
    
- To Roman Soldiers: In Mark 15:19, Roman soldiers mockingly fall on their knees and "worshipped" (proskuneo) Jesus as a mock political king. [20, 21, 22, 23]

---

#### Unlawful vs. Lawful Non-Divine Worship

The Bible draws a sharp line regarding when bowing to someone or something other than God is acceptable: [24]

|Type of Devotion|Action & Context|Biblical Stance|
|---|---|---|
|Civil Homage|Bowing out of respect to kings, parents, or prophets (shachah / proskuneo).|Lawful (Considered a standard cultural sign of honor).|
|Idolatry|Bowing to statues, false gods, cosmic bodies, or demons (latreuo / religious shachah).|Strictly Forbidden (Violates the 1st and 2nd Commandments).|
|Angelic Adoration|Prostrating oneself out of fear or religious awe before an angel.|Forbidden (Angels consistently refuse it, stating they are fellow servants; see Revelation 19:10).|

#### Cultic Service (Latreuo)

Crucially, while shachah and proskuneo can be directed toward humans or God depending on intent, the Greek word latreuo (religious, sacrificial temple service) is reserved exclusively for God in the New Testament. When Satan asked Jesus for proskuneo (a bow of submission), Jesus countered by stating that humans must only offer latreuo (sacred service) to God alone (Matthew 4:10). [2, 25]

## Both towards God and king David
Yes, in 1 Chronicles 29:20, the exact same Hebrew word—shachah (שָׁחָה)—is used for the actions directed toward both God and King David. [1]

In fact, this verse uses two physical posture words back-to-back to describe the intense reverence of the assembly: [1, 2]

1. Wayyiqqəḏū (וַיִּקְּדוּ): "And they bowed their heads" (from qadad).
    
2. Wayyištaḥăwū (וַיִּשְׁתַּחֲוּּ): "And they prostrated themselves / worshiped" (from shachah). [1, 2, 3]

The Hebrew text phrases it as a single physical act directed toward two different entities: [1]

"...and [they] bowed down their heads, and prostrated themselves (shachah) before the LORD and the king." [1, 4]

#### How Translations Handle It

Because the English word "worship" has a strictly religious meaning today, older and newer translations translate this joint action differently: [1, 5]

- Older Translations (like the King James Version): Render it directly as "...and worshipped the LORD, and the king." In 1611, the English word "worship" still carried its original etymological meaning of "worth-ship"—giving honor or acknowledging the worth of someone in authority (which is why British judges are still addressed as "Your Worship").
    
- Modern Translations (like the ESV, NASB, or NIV): To avoid the impression that the Israelites were treating King David as a divine being, they translate the physical action: "...and bowed low and did homage to the LORD and to the king." [4, 5, 6, 7]

This verse serves as an excellent example of how shachah describes a physical posture of deep submission and respect that could be applied vertically to God as deity, and horizontally to a human ruler as a civic authority figure. [1, 5, 8]
